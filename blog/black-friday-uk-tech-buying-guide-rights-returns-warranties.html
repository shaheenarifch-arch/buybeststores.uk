Quick answer

Black Friday falls on 27 November 2026, with Cyber Monday on 30 November. The discounts change, but your legal rights do not. If you buy tech online from a UK business, you normally have 14 days from delivery to change your mind and cancel. If something turns out to be faulty, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 gives you 30 days to reject it for a full refund, then a repair-or-replace stage, and claims can be brought for up to six years (five in Scotland). Pay by credit card for single items costing over £100 and you also get Section 75 protection. For bulky items such as treadmills and walking pads, check the return costs, delivery terms and your space before you click buy.

Key takeaways

  • A Black Friday price does not reduce your rights. Sale items from a UK business are covered by the same consumer law as full-price items.
  • The online cancellation right and the faulty-goods rights are different things with different clocks.
  • Your contract is with the retailer, so faulty-goods claims go to the shop first, even if the manufacturer also offers a warranty.
  • For treadmills and other heavy kit, delivery, packaging and return costs matter as much as the headline discount.

Why Black Friday tech buying needs a plan

UK retailers now treat the whole of late November as a sales season. Offers often start well before the Friday itself and run through Cyber Monday and beyond. That gives you room to compare, but it also creates pressure: countdown timers, "limited stock" banners and bundles that are hard to compare with anything else.

Decide what you actually need before the offers land. Write down the product type, the features that matter to you and the most you are willing to spend. For a tablet that might be storage and screen size; for a walking pad it might be the stated weight capacity, the deck size and whether it folds. When an offer appears, you can check it against your list in seconds instead of being steered by whatever is promoted most heavily that morning.

Online versus in-store: the 14-day cancellation right

Most Black Friday tech is bought online, and that matters. When you buy from a UK business at a distance (online, by phone or by mail order), the Consumer Contracts Regulations give you a cooling-off period. You can cancel for any reason, which is useful when a laptop turns out to be heavier than you expected or a treadmill does not fit the space you measured.

When does the 14-day clock start?

For goods, the cancellation period ends 14 days after the day you receive them, and if an order arrives in several parts, it generally runs from the day the last item arrives. That helps during Black Friday, when delivery can be slower: a treadmill ordered on 27 November but delivered in mid-December still gives you 14 days from delivery.

Once you have told the retailer you are cancelling, you normally have a further 14 days to send the goods back. The retailer must refund you within 14 days of receiving the goods, or of you providing evidence that you have sent them, whichever is sooner. The refund should include the standard delivery charge, but not any extra you paid for faster delivery.

Can you use the item during those 14 days?

You can handle and inspect it as you would in a shop, to establish what it is and how it works. If you go further, the retailer may reduce your refund to reflect the loss in value. Switching on a tablet to check the screen is reasonable; using it for weeks is not. For a walking pad, checking that it powers on and runs is reasonable, but scuffing the belt with regular workouts is likely to reduce what you get back.

Who pays to send it back?

If the retailer told you before you bought that you would pay return costs, you usually do; if it did not, it generally has to cover them. For goods that cannot normally be sent back by post because of their size, the retailer is expected to give you an estimate of the return cost before you buy. That matters for treadmills: a free-delivery headline can be followed by a costly collection if you change your mind, so look for the returns information or ask before ordering.

What is not covered?

Exclusions include goods made to your personal specification, sealed audio, video or software you have unsealed, and digital content once you have agreed to the download starting. Standard hardware such as phones, smartwatches and treadmills is usually covered, although sealed hygiene-related items such as in-ear headphones can be treated differently once unsealed.

When something goes wrong: faulty goods under the Consumer Rights Act

The cancellation right is about changing your mind. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 is about goods that are not right. Goods bought from a business must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described, and that includes the product page. If a listing says a treadmill has a particular incline or weight capacity and it does not, that can be a breach.

The first 30 days

If a fault shows up within 30 days, you can reject the item for a full refund. You do not have to accept a repair or replacement if you would rather have your money back, and the refund should come within 14 days of the retailer agreeing you are entitled to it.

From 30 days to six months

After 30 days, the retailer gets one opportunity to repair or replace the item. If that does not fix the problem, you can ask for a refund or a price reduction. During the first six months, a fault is presumed to have been there at delivery unless the retailer can show otherwise, and a refund in this period generally cannot be reduced for the use you have had.

After six months

You can still claim, but you usually need to show that the fault was present at delivery rather than caused by wear and tear or misuse. Claims can be brought for up to six years in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and five in Scotland. That is not a six-year guarantee: a budget item is not expected to last as long as a premium one.

Warranties, guarantees and extended cover

Black Friday listings for phones, laptops and fitness equipment often highlight warranties. They can be valuable, but it helps to understand where they sit.

Manufacturer warranty versus your legal rights

A manufacturer's warranty is an extra promise from the brand. It does not replace your legal rights against the retailer. If a treadmill motor fails in month four, you can use the warranty if that is quicker, or go to the retailer under the Consumer Rights Act. Some warranties need registering within a set period, so check the paperwork when the item arrives.

Is extended cover worth it?

Before adding an extended warranty or protection plan at checkout, check what it covers that your legal rights and the manufacturer warranty do not, such as accidental damage. Also check whether your home contents insurance or a bank account package already covers gadgets. Overlapping cover is an easy way to add cost to a deal without noticing.

Paying safely: credit cards, debit cards and buy now pay later

Section 75 on credit cards

If you pay by credit card for a single item with a cash price over £100 and up to £30,000, Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes the card provider jointly liable with the retailer for breach of contract or misrepresentation. If the retailer goes out of business or refuses a valid claim, you can go to your card provider instead. It applies even if you only paid part of the price on the card, but paying through some third-party payment services can break the link and remove this protection.

Chargeback on debit and credit cards

Chargeback is not a law but a process run under card scheme rules. It can help if goods do not arrive or are not as described, including on smaller amounts, but time limits apply, so contact your bank promptly.

How to tell whether a Black Friday deal is genuine

A discount is only useful if the original price was real and the product suits you. UK rules on unfair commercial practices mean retailers should not use misleading reference prices, and since April 2025 the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has given the Competition and Markets Authority stronger direct enforcement powers, along with specific bans on fake reviews and on hiding unavoidable fees until late in the checkout. Your own checks still matter.

Check the "was" price

A "was" price should reflect a genuine earlier selling price. If you have been watching a product, you will know roughly what it normally costs. If not, compare the same model at two or three retailers, and be wary of a reference price you have never seen it sold at.

Look at the full cost

Add delivery, any assembly charge, the accessories you will genuinely need and any cover you plan to buy. A smaller discount with free delivery to your room can beat a larger headline saving with paid kerbside delivery.

Treadmills and walking pads: a Black Friday checklist

Winter is when many people in the UK think about exercising indoors, so treadmills and walking pads feature heavily in late November promotions. They are also among the most awkward items to return, so a few extra checks are worthwhile.

  1. Measure twice. Measure the floor space where you will use it, the ceiling height if you plan to use incline or run, the space under your desk for an under-desk model, and where it will be stored when folded. Also measure doorways, stairs and lifts on the way in.
  2. Check the stated weight capacity. Make sure your body weight sits comfortably within the stated maximum. Our guide to folding walking pad weight capacity compares the stated figures for several DeerRun UK models.
  3. Understand the delivery. Find out whether delivery is to the kerb, the door or a room of your choice. Carrying a heavy box up several flights of stairs is a real consideration in a flat.
  4. Keep the packaging. Until your cancellation period has passed and you are happy, keep the box and packing materials. Returning a bulky item without them is harder.
  5. Ask about return costs in advance. If you cannot find the estimated return cost for an item that cannot be posted, ask before ordering.

Marketplaces, third-party sellers and overseas shops

Many Black Friday offers on large marketplaces come from third-party sellers. Your rights are normally against the seller, so check who that is on the listing before you buy.

  • Business sellers: the Consumer Rights Act and, for online sales, the cancellation right generally apply as with any UK retailer.
  • Private sellers: buying from an individual gives you far fewer rights. The item must be as described, but the quality and fitness rules do not apply in the same way, and there is no 14-day cancellation right.
  • Sellers outside the UK: enforcing your rights can be much harder, returns can be expensive and import charges may apply. For electrical goods, check the item comes with a UK plug or suitable charger.

What to do if something goes wrong

  1. Contact the retailer in writing. Give your order number, describe the problem, say what you want (a refund, repair or replacement) and, where it helps, mention the Consumer Rights Act 2015 or the Consumer Contracts Regulations.
  2. Keep evidence. Photos of the fault, screenshots of the listing, delivery notes and dates all help.
  3. Use your payment protection. For eligible credit card purchases, make a Section 75 claim; otherwise ask your bank about chargeback.
  4. Get independent advice. Citizens Advice offers free consumer advice, including on alternative dispute resolution and, as a last resort, the small claims process.

Limitations and checks

Specifications, prices, delivery terms and returns policies can change at any time, especially during sales, so check the current product page before ordering. Bottom line

Black Friday can be a sensible time to buy tech or fitness equipment you already planned to buy. Buy online from a UK business and you get 14 days to change your mind. Buy something faulty and the Consumer Rights Act gives you a refund, repair or replacement. Pay by credit card for items over £100 and Section 75 adds a second line of protection. For treadmills and walking pads, measure your space, check the stated weight capacity, understand the delivery and return costs, and keep the packaging until you are sure.

Frequently asked questions

Do my consumer rights still apply to Black Friday sale items?

Yes. Goods bought from a UK business in a sale are covered by the same consumer law as full-price goods. They must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described, and online purchases usually come with the 14-day cancellation right. The main exception is a fault you were told about before buying, or one you should have noticed when examining the item.

How long do I have to return something I bought online on Black Friday?

For most goods bought online from a UK business, you can cancel within 14 days starting the day after delivery, then you normally have a further 14 days to send the item back. The retailer must refund you, including standard delivery, within 14 days of receiving the goods or proof that you have sent them.

Can I return a treadmill if it does not fit my space?

If you bought it online from a UK business, you can usually cancel within the 14-day period even if nothing is wrong with it. You may have to pay the return cost if the retailer told you so before you bought, and for bulky items the retailer should give an estimate of that cost in advance. Keep the packaging and avoid using it beyond checking that it works.

Does a manufacturer warranty replace my rights under the Consumer Rights Act?

No. A manufacturer warranty is an extra promise from the brand. Your legal rights for faulty goods are against the retailer, and they apply whether or not there is a warranty. You can choose whichever route is easier, but the retailer cannot refuse a valid claim by telling you to contact the manufacturer.

Is it better to pay by credit card during Black Friday?

For single items with a cash price over £100 and up to £30,000, paying by credit card adds Section 75 protection, which makes the card provider jointly responsible if something goes wrong with the purchase. Only use credit if you can repay it, and note that paying through some third-party payment services can remove this protection.

What if I bought from a marketplace seller based outside the UK?

Your rights are against the seller, and enforcing them against a business outside the UK can be difficult in practice. Returns may be costly and import charges can apply. For electrical items, check that the product is intended for the UK market and comes with a UK plug or suitable charger before you order.
